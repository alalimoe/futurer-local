Stack Architecture

More isn’t always better.

It’s easy to build a stack by addition.

One compound works well.

So you add another.

Then something for support.

Then something to smooth it out.

Then something else because… maybe.

Eventually you have six things doing three jobs.

And no clear idea which one is actually helping.

Start with the job.

Before adding anything, ask:

What is this supposed to do?

Not “is this compound interesting?”

What role does it have in the setup?

A cleaner stack usually has a simple hierarchy:

 

Primary

The main reason the stack exists.
 
 

Support

Something that complements the primary without competing for the same job.
 
 

Optional

Something you can add when the specific day or situation calls for it.

That’s enough more often than people think.

Overlap adds noise.

If three compounds are all there because you want “more focus,” you haven’t necessarily built a stronger stack.

You may have just made it harder to read.

Because when the setup feels great:

What worked?
 

And when it doesn’t:

What caused the problem?

If five variables changed at once, both questions become difficult to answer.

Try the removal test.

Look at your current setup.

Pick one ingredient and ask:

If I removed this, what specific function would disappear?

If the answer isn’t obvious, that ingredient may not be earning its place.

The goal isn’t the fewest compounds possible.

It’s the fewest that clearly justify being there.

That makes your setup easier to understand, easier to adjust, and easier to repeat when you find something that works for you.

Build backwards.

Start with the day.

What are you actually trying to accomplish?

Deep work?

Long-output work?

Learning?

A calmer evening?

A simple daily baseline?

Choose the primary tool from there.

Then add support only when you can explain why it’s there.

Not because a stack diagram told you to.

Not because another bottle is sitting on the shelf.

Not because four compounds feels more “advanced” than two.

Sometimes the more experienced move is subtraction.

Less noise. More intention.
